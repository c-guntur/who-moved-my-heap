== Shakers: memory allocated per call

=== What each call leaves behind

From the six per-class analyses (links in <<docs>>).

. *The JDK stream leaves 6 to 8 objects (160 to 224 B) behind on every call; every Eclipse Collections (EC)
  variant leaves none.* Measured by `gc.alloc.rate.norm` and confirmed object by object with the class
  histogram (all 702 measurement iterations across the three histogram runs were GC-free). At 10 million
  calls per second across an application, that is 1.6 to 2.2 GB/s of young-generation churn for the stream
  versions and effectively 0 for EC.
. *Heap headroom for spikes (derived):* a 10-second spike at 1 million calls/s needs 1.6 to 2.2 GB of extra
  young generation for the stream versions to get through without an extra collection, and none for EC.
. *Fat-free lambdas matter when escape analysis cannot help.* With escape analysis on, EC's capturing and
  `*With` calls both allocate nothing. With it off (`-XX:-DoEscapeAnalysis`, an upper bound), the capturing
  `Iterate.anySatisfy` allocates 16 to 32 B per call and the fat-free `Iterate.anySatisfyWith` 0 to 16 B: the
  only remaining object is the `Integer` box from autoboxing the `int` parameter.
. *Compact object headers trim the JDK garbage by 16 to 24 B per call (7 to 11%)*, with the same number of
  objects (per-class histogram), because only objects that cross an 8-byte boundary shrink. Not allocating
  saves the whole 160 to 224 B.
. *Throughput:* EC is about 11 to 17x faster at `test` = 1 (stream setup dominates), 4 to 6x at
  `test` = 500/1000, and about 2x for `count`, which always scans all 1000 elements.
. *Unexpected:* the EC loops ran 8 to 25% *faster* with compact headers off on this machine (`test` =
  500/1000). Inferred cause: `Integer.equals` checks every element's class, and with compact headers the
  class pointer has to be decoded from the header word. Check this on the local Mac before using it.
. The earlier run's ±35% error bars on `jdkCount` did not reproduce (±6% here).

=== Where the JDK garbage comes from (JDK Stream ops)

From link:shakers/20261015-0406-JdkStreamOpsAnalysis.adoc[the JDK Stream ops analysis].

. *The garbage is fixed per call, not per element:* the pipeline is built and thrown away on every call,
  wherever the match is.
. *More than a third of it is the Stream source.* `list.stream()` alone costs 80 B (pipeline head 48 B +
  spliterator 32 B) before any operation is chosen.
. *The terminal op differs most:*
  * `anyMatch` (MatchOps) allocates 3 objects, 64 B: `MatchOps.makeRef` builds a new `MatchOp` and a new
    sink-supplier lambda on every call.
  * `findFirst` (FindOps) allocates 2 objects, 32 B: the `FindOp` is a cached static, but the sink and the
    `Optional` result are new.
  * `count` (ReduceOps) allocates 2 objects, 40 B: a new `ReduceOps$5` op on every call plus the counting sink.
. *`filter` adds a whole pipeline stage* (2 objects, 72 B). That is why `stream().anyMatch(p)` (160 B) is
  cheaper than `stream().filter(p).findFirst()` (200 B) for the same question.
. *Escape analysis removes none of it:* bytes per call are identical with `-XX:-DoEscapeAnalysis` for every
  JDK benchmark. The captured lambda (16 B) and the `Integer` box (16 B at `test` = 500/1000) come on top and
  escape into the pipeline.
. *Speed at match position 1*, where almost nothing is scanned: a JDK call takes 29 to 38 ns against 2 to
  3 ns for EC. Building and dropping these objects is most of that difference (inferred).

=== The same operations in Eclipse Collections

. *EC has no pipeline to build:* its methods are a loop over the list that calls the predicate, so the
  Stream source, filter stage and terminal op have no EC counterpart. On a normal JVM every EC version,
  capturing or fat-free, eager or lazy, allocates 0 B and needed 0 garbage collections.
. *With escape analysis off, the lambda shape shows:* a fat-free `Iterate.*With` call leaves only the
  `Integer` box the caller passes in (16 B, 0 B at element 1); a capturing call adds its lambda (32 B);
  `asLazy()` adds a `LazyIterableAdapter`, and `asLazy().count` also a `CountProcedure`; the JDK stream
  leaves 176 to 224 B.
. *Surprise worth raising with EC:* on an `ImmutableList` in EC 13.0.0, `anySatisfyWith`, `countWith` and
  `detectWithIfNone` are inherited from `AbstractRichIterable`, which wraps the call in
  `Predicates.bind(...)`, so they are not fat-free: with escape analysis off they allocate as much as the
  capturing versions. Only `detectWithOptional` is overridden in `ImmutableArrayList` and stays fat-free.
  The `Iterate.*With` utilities loop directly and do not have this problem (checked in the EC 13.0.0 bytecode).

== Movers: memory footprint (JOL)

Retained size of a filled collection or an unevaluated pipeline (`GraphLayout.totalSize()`), not
allocation or speed. Every figure is at 1,000,000 elements with compact headers on unless stated.

=== Lists

From link:movers/20261015-1503-ListMemoryAnalysis.adoc[the list analysis].

. *Boxing is the cost:* a presized `ArrayList` or `FastList` holds 20.0 MB; `IntArrayList` holds 4.0 MB, 5x
  less (20 B per element against 4 B, derived).
. `ArrayList` and `FastList` are the same size, within 8 B, when presized.
. *Growing instead of presizing wastes up to 49%* (unused capacity after the last resize).
. Compact headers change almost nothing: a 16 B `Integer` stays 16 B.

=== Sets

From link:movers/20261015-1503-SetMemoryAnalysis.adoc[the set analysis].

. *`HashSet` holds twice as much as `UnifiedSet`:* 48.4 MB against 24.4 MB; `IntHashSet` holds 8.4 MB,
  about 6x less than `HashSet`.
. *Without the `Integer`s the gap is 4x:* 32.4 MB against 8.4 MB, because `HashSet` makes a `HashMap$Node`
  per element (24 B with compact headers, 32 B without).
. *Compact headers save 8.0 MB on `HashSet`* and nothing on the EC sets.

=== Maps

From link:movers/20261015-1503-MapMemoryAnalysis.adoc[the map analysis].

. *`HashMap<Integer, Integer>` holds 48.4 MB; `UnifiedMap` holds 32.8 MB; `IntIntHashMap` holds 16.8 MB*,
  about 3x less than `HashMap`.
. *Without the `Integer`s:* `HashMap` takes 32.4 MB, `UnifiedMap` 16.8 MB (key and value side by side in
  one `Object[]`, no per-entry objects).
. *Half-primitive maps do not help here:* `IntObjectHashMap` and `ObjectIntHashMap` hold 32.8 MB, the same
  as `UnifiedMap`, because one side is still a boxed `Integer`.
. *Compact headers save 8.0 MB on `HashMap`* (56.4 MB off, 48.4 MB on) and nothing on the EC maps.

=== Stream vs LazyIterable pipelines

From link:movers/20261015-1503-StreamVsLazyIterableMemoryAnalysis.adoc[the pipeline analysis].

. *Starting is 5x cheaper in EC:* `list.stream()` is 80 B, the same 80 B the shakers object counts measured
  per call; `ecList.asLazy()` is 16 B.
. *Each JDK operation adds 48 to 72 B; each EC operation 0 to 48 B.* The 3-step chain is 272 B against 96 B.
. These are pipeline graph sizes, not bytes per call: JOL counts the shared, non-capturing lambdas. For bytes
  per call, see the JDK Stream ops subsection above.
